# How to read

Of the review rules, `coding/leave-harmless-redundancy-out-of-review-findings.md` applies to a hunt. The others (`coding/report-data-safety-findings-before-style.md`, `coding/fix-mechanical-findings-without-asking.md`, and `coding/handle-a-new-enum-value-in-every-consumer.md`) govern reviews of a change.

- **Argue every finding, and say which kind it is** (proven or judgement, as "Proven and judgement" below defines them). A finding you can't argue as proven or as judgement stays out. Never pad the list with what's easy to prove: a ten-line cleanup that's certain matters less than a 500-line subsystem that's doubtful, and the ranking says so.
- **Weigh before you read lines.** Before you go file by file, write down what each piece of what you cover is for and how many lines it takes (`sizes.txt` in the hunt folder gives sizes). Ask of each: what does it give users, what's the cheapest shape that gives them that (the platform, a library, the host's or caller's job, a smaller design, or nothing), and what would they lose? Then read the files, and let what you read confirm or overturn the weighing. This goes in your report's weight map.
- **Follow the work, not the folder.** Start at the entry points (endpoints, message handlers, jobs, pages), follow one request down to persistence, then read what's left. Slop shows up as a layer the request passes through without anything happening to it, and as machinery that exists to serve other machinery.
- **Search the whole repo, not just your area,** before you claim that something is duplicated, unused, or missing (sign 6, Dead code, lists what a symbol search misses).
- **Never claim to have read what you didn't.** A hunter reads every file of its area in full: no skimming, sampling, or reading signatures in place of bodies. A hunter that runs out of room before it has read them all names the files it didn't read on the last line of its report ("The hunters' report", below), and never leaves a file off that line that it didn't read in full. The design reader's report has no such line, and it never claims a file it didn't read.
- **Ask two questions of every finding.** *Necessity:* what breaks if this goes? Name the behaviour a user would see, or say "nothing". "A test fails" isn't a behaviour. An error message's wording, or an edge no real user meets, does count, but say it plainly so it can be weighed. *History:* why is it here? `git log -L <start>,<end>:<file> -s --format='%h %as %s'` lists the commits that touched those lines; `git show <hash>` reads the one that matters. Cite its hash when the lines are a guard or a copy. A guard that a bug-fix commit added is a fence: say what replaces it, or show that the bug can no longer happen.
- **A recorded decision is evidence, not a verdict.** When a spec Decision, the repo's instructions, a doc, or a commit chose this shape, cite it. If the cost case still holds, report the finding as judgement and say which decision it reopens: the user decides. "The repo chose this" never ends the argument by itself.
- **Name what still protects it.** A protection is whatever stops a behaviour from breaking unnoticed: a test, a guard, a validator, a constraint, or a type. A finding that removes or weakens one names, by `file:line`, what still protects that behaviour afterwards, and that successor must not itself be something you're flagging. When nothing does and the behaviour is meant to stay, it isn't slop. A design finding that drops a behaviour on purpose names the tests that pin it instead: they change with it. When one finding is only true once another lands (B is dead only once A goes), report them as one finding.
- **Don't hunt bugs.** You're looking for weight, not defects. A bug you trip over gets one line under Also noticed ("The hunters' report", below), with its `file:line`, and no proof, history, or fix work. Don't go looking for more. A broken house Law that a sign below covers isn't a bug for this purpose: it's a finding under that sign, with its proof.
- **Leads aren't findings.** A knip, jscpd, or analyzer hit is a place to read, and so is a design lead from the hunt's design reader. A lead becomes a finding only once it has been checked against the code. A hunter whose area holds code that a design lead covers adds what it sees there to the lead (confirm it, price it more exactly, or contradict it, citing `file:line`) rather than reporting the same thing again.
